Since iOS 11, every photo you take on an iPhone is saved as HEIC — not JPEG. Most iPhone users never notice. Until they try to open those photos on Windows, upload them to a website, or email them to someone with an older phone.

Why did Apple make this change? And what should you do about it?

## The Storage Problem

iPhone cameras kept getting better. More megapixels, better sensors, Live Photos, Portrait mode. Each improvement meant larger files.

JPEG, the decades-old standard, couldn't keep up. A 12-megapixel JPEG might be 3–5MB. Multiply by thousands of photos and your 64GB iPhone fills fast.

Apple needed a format that stored more photos in less space without visible quality loss.

## Enter HEIC

**HEIC** (High Efficiency Image Container) uses **HEVC** (H.265) compression — the same technology behind efficient video streaming. Compared to JPEG at the same visual quality:

- **Roughly half the file size**
- **Better quality** at equivalent compression levels
- **Support for transparency** (like PNG)
- **Support for multiple images** in one file (Live Photos, burst sequences)

Your iPhone stores twice as many photos in the same space. That's the main reason Apple switched.

## The Compatibility Tradeoff

HEIC is better technically. But JPEG is universal. Windows, older Android phones, many websites, and legacy software expect `.jpg` files.

When you AirDrop a HEIC photo to a Windows PC, it often fails to open. When you upload HEIC to a form that only accepts JPEG, you get an error. When you email HEIC to a colleague, they might not see the attachment correctly.

Apple partially solves this: when you share via Messages or Mail, iOS often converts to JPEG automatically. But file transfers, cloud sync, and manual exports may not.

## Your Options

### Keep HEIC (save space on iPhone)

Stay with the default. Convert only when you need compatibility — for a specific upload, email, or Windows transfer.

### Switch to JPEG (maximum compatibility)

**Settings → Camera → Formats → Most Compatible.** New photos are JPEG. Existing HEIC photos stay HEIC until you convert them.

### Convert when needed

Use a converter for batch export — HEIC to JPEG for a folder of photos you're archiving or sharing. Browser-based conversion keeps your photos on your device.

## HEIC on the Web

Web browsers are adding HEIC support slowly. For websites and apps, converting to WebP or JPEG before upload is still the safe choice.
