You need to submit a signed form as PDF. You don't have a scanner. You take three photos with your phone — but the recipient wants one document, not three JPEG attachments.

This is one of the most common PDF workflows, and it doesn't require a dedicated scanning app or uploading photos to a server.

## When Photo-to-PDF Makes Sense

- **Receipts and invoices** for expense reports
- **Signed forms** — permission slips, contracts, applications
- **Handwritten notes** you want to archive digitally
- **Whiteboard captures** from meetings
- **ID or document copies** for verification (keep these local — never upload sensitive IDs to random websites)
- **Recipe cards, instructions, or reference sheets**

## Step 1: Take Better Photos

You don't need a scanner-quality setup, but a few habits help:

- **Good lighting** — avoid shadows across the document
- **Flat surface** — reduce perspective distortion
- **Fill the frame** — document edges near the photo edges
- **Hold steady** — blur ruins text readability
- **One page per photo** — easier to reorder later

Most phone cameras are good enough. The bottleneck is usually lighting, not megapixels.

## Step 2: Convert Images to PDF

The workflow is straightforward:

1. Select your photos (JPEG, HEIC, or PNG)
2. Arrange them in page order
3. Generate a single PDF
4. Download

Each photo becomes one PDF page. A five-photo receipt stack becomes a five-page PDF.

## Step 3: Optimize If Needed

Phone photos can be large — 3–8MB each. A 10-page PDF might hit 50MB, too big for email.

**Resize first:** Documents don't need 4000px resolution. 1500–2000px on the long edge is plenty for readable text.

**Compress:** Reduce JPEG quality slightly — quality 80 is fine for document photos.

**Convert HEIC:** iPhone photos in HEIC format may not open everywhere. Convert to JPEG as part of the workflow.

## Privacy: Why Local Processing Matters

Scanning apps and "photo to PDF" websites often upload your photos to cloud servers for processing — sometimes for OCR, sometimes for storage, sometimes for "AI enhancement."

If you're photographing contracts, medical forms, financial statements, or ID documents, that upload is a serious privacy risk. Browser-based conversion keeps photos on your device.

## Common Mistakes

**Wrong page order** — double-check before generating. Reordering after is annoying.

**Including your desk/floor in the frame** — crop mentally when shooting, or crop before converting.

**Using server tools for sensitive docs** — bank statements and tax forms should never leave your device.

**Forgetting HEIC compatibility** — Windows users and many PDF workflows need JPEG or PNG, not HEIC.
