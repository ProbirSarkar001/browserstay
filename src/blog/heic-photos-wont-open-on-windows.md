You transferred photos from your iPhone to your Windows PC. You double-click one and get an error: "Windows cannot open this file" or "You'll need a new app to open this .heic file."

Your photos aren't broken. Windows just doesn't understand Apple's default format.

## What Is HEIC?

**HEIC** (High Efficiency Image Container) is the default photo format on iPhones since iOS 11. Apple switched because HEIC files are roughly half the size of JPEG at the same visual quality — your phone stores twice as many photos in the same space.

HEIC uses **HEVC** (H.265) compression, the same technology behind high-efficiency video. It's genuinely better than JPEG for most photos.

The catch: Windows, older Android phones, and many websites don't support it out of the box.

## Why Windows Struggles

Microsoft added HEIC support in Windows 10 and 11, but it requires an optional codec from the Microsoft Store — and it's not installed by default. Many users never know it exists.

Without that codec, `.heic` files are invisible to File Explorer previews, Paint, and most apps.

## Your Options

### Option 1: Install the HEIC Codec (Windows)

Search the Microsoft Store for "HEIF Image Extensions" (free) and "HEVC Video Extensions" (a paid item, around $0.99). Install both. After that, Windows can open and preview HEIC files natively.

Downside: HEVC Video Extensions isn't free, and you're installing Microsoft codecs; some users prefer not to.

### Option 2: Change iPhone Settings to Shoot JPEG

On your iPhone: **Settings → Camera → Formats → Most Compatible.** New photos will be JPEG. Existing HEIC photos stay HEIC.

Downside: larger file sizes on your phone going forward.

### Option 3: Convert HEIC to JPEG

Convert your existing HEIC files to JPEG (or PNG) so they work everywhere — email, websites, older software, printing services.

You can convert one photo or batch-convert hundreds.

## JPEG vs PNG for Conversion

- **JPEG** — smaller files, universal support. Best for photos.
- **PNG** — lossless, larger files. Best if you need perfect quality or transparency (rare for phone photos).

For everyday iPhone photos, JPEG is the right choice.
